Inside Gridiron Odds

Every morning a computer downloads last night’s NFL data, re-learns which teams are good, predicts every upcoming game, and rewrites the entire website as a single file.

No database. No app server. No AI service. About 6,800 lines of Python and one rented Linux machine.

in Free NFL data Every play since 1999 — roughly a million of them — plus weather and live scores.
6:30am daily Rebuild everything Re-rate all 32 teams, retrain the model from scratch, predict every game. Takes 40 seconds.
out One HTML file 573 KB with the predictions baked inside. That file is the website.

Where the numbers come from

Four sources. Only one of them costs anything.

nflverse A volunteer project publishing every NFL play since 1999 — score, weather, injuries, who was on the field for which snap. This is the foundation of the whole app. free · no account
ESPN Quarterback ratings, and live scores during games. Its public scoreboard is what powers the live win-probability bar. free · no key
Open-Meteo Weather forecasts, so an outdoor game in 20 mph wind gets predicted differently from one under a roof. free · no key
The Odds API Betting lines, used as a benchmark to measure against. Pulled only Wednesdays and Sundays to keep the bill down. the only paid one

The two brains

Predictions come from mixing two completely different methods, because each one is wrong in different places.

Elo — 60% of the answer The chess rating system, borrowed for football. Every team carries a number near 1500. Beat someone better than you and your number goes up, theirs goes down. Simple, and stubbornly hard to beat.
XGBoost — 40% of the answer Machine learning that builds thousands of tiny yes/no questions and stacks them. Higher rated? Quarterback playing? Windy? Each is dumb alone; thousands together get smart. It sees ~70 facts per game.

Why 60/40? It was tested, not guessed. Every blend from pure Elo to pure XGBoost was scored against eleven seasons of real results, and 60/40 produced the most honest probabilities — when it says 70%, that team really does win about 70% of the time.

Result: right on 65% of games. The Vegas closing line gets 66%. On the picks it flags as high-confidence, it hits 73%.

The 6:30am routine

A scheduled job (refresh.sh) runs these in order every morning. It runs again Sunday at 11:45am ET to catch injury news that lands just before kickoff.

  1. 01fetch_spread_odds.py Grab the current Vegas lines.
  2. 02build_clutch.py How each team performs in the last five minutes of close games.
  3. 03build_qb_splits.py Quarterback performance broken out by quarter.
  4. 04build_qbr.py Pull ESPN’s official quarterback ratings.
  5. 05features.py The heart of it. 1,000 lines that turn raw plays into “here’s how good every team and player is right now.”
  6. 06train.py Re-learn from scratch on everything through last night.
  7. 07props.py Predict individual player yardage.
  8. 08export_web.py Write the website.

The website is one file

This is the unusual part, and it is a deliberate choice. There is no database, no backend, and no application server. The entire site is three files sitting in a folder:

/var/www/nfl/
  index.html   573 KB — the whole site, data baked inside
  live.json      2 B — live scores, empty between games
  og.jpg        71 KB — preview image for texts and social

export_web.py takes every prediction, turns it into a block of data, and pastes it inside the HTML. A visitor downloads that one file and their browser draws the charts.

Why this is smart: there is nothing to crash. No database to go down, no server code to break. Serving the site is as reliable as serving a photo, and it loads fast anywhere.

Notably absent: no React, no database, no cloud platform, no AI API. The world model is decision trees and lookup tables — it is not a language model and makes no API calls, so it costs nothing per run.

What the world model was tested on

Five ideas, each measured against real games the model had never seen. Four failed. Failing them on purpose is what keeps the site honest.

IdeaResultVerdict
Better pregame picks Gain too small to be real no
Better spread picks 53% vs the current 55% no
Better spread uncertainty Measurably worse no
Explaining injuries Overstates by 3–6× no
Live win probability Ties the best free version that peeks at the betting line shipped

Why most of it failed. Elo is built from final scores — and final scores are just what all those drives added up to. So simulating the drives underneath told Elo almost nothing it had not already learned. The one place it wins is during a game, because a pregame prediction has nothing to say about 3rd and 7 with two minutes left.
